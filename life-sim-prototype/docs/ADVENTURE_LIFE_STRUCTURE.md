# Adventure + Life Sim Structure

Set several years after Rolling Tides, in the Merging Era: the Dream Realm has been exposed and is beginning to
overlap with physical reality. This game leans much harder on exploration, travel, environmental puzzles, ancient
mysteries, discovering civilizations, player-directed journeys and eventually cosmic exploration.

**The player is not required to become a cosmic hero.** The structural philosophy is:
*go as far as you want, build a life wherever you stop.* One player may spend the whole game between ordinary towns —
relationships, work, sports, shopping, money, family. Another may follow every mystery past the known world. Both are
correct.

## 1. Two fantasies, neither secondary
- **Life:** "I want to live in this world."
- **Adventure:** "I want to discover how big this world really is."

## 2. The life-sim world
Towns and cities are places to live, not quest hubs. Players can rent or own homes, decorate, work, earn, shop, cook,
eat, sleep, exercise, skateboard, play basketball and other sports, play music, attend events, build friendships,
date, marry, raise children, develop skills, collect clothing, customize appearance, meet and invite NPCs, travel
recreationally and explore without touching the main mystery.

NPCs have lives too: work, travel, socializing, homes, relationships, activities, locations, reactions to world
events. The world should feel worth saving because it is worth living in.

## 3. Towns are destinations
No settlement exists only because "Quest 14 happens here". Each needs its own architecture, clothing, music, food,
activities, sports, jobs, stores, transport, nightlife, NPC culture, racial demographics, local history, surrounding
wilderness and nearby mysteries. The player should sometimes travel somewhere just because they like that town.

## 4. Travel is gameplay
Not teleporting between objective markers. Walking, running, skateboarding, bicycles, cars, buses, trains, boats,
mounts, fantasy transport, ancient transport systems, Dream pathways, eventually interplanetary travel. Travel
exposes the player to NPC encounters, roadside locations, secrets, environmental puzzles, camps, random events,
merchants, wildlife, enemies, Merged Zones and hidden communities. Fast travel may exist but must not erase the value
of moving through the world.

## 5. Adventure escalation
Local ("something strange outside town") → regional → national/continental → ancient → Dream → planetary → other
worlds → cosmic. The player begins as a person, not a chosen one.

## 6. Player-driven
Mysteries are presented without forcing participation: investigate now, bring friends later, ignore it and go to
work, play basketball, or travel elsewhere. The ruins stay part of the world; curiosity decides the depth.

## 7. The mystery ladder
Discoveries create optional deeper questions: "cool artifact" → "where did it come from?" → "these symbols appear in
another region" → "they match something in the Dream Realm" → "the Dream version is newer" → "because neither
civilization made it". Curiosity itself functions as progression.

## 8. Puzzles
Much more important than in Rolling Tides, and not just push-block/find-key/pull-lever/match-symbols. Categories:
environmental (architecture, shadows, water, terrain, machinery), physical (move, climb, jump, redirect, construct,
destroy), mechanical (repair ancient machines — gear path), magical (arcane systems), biological (living systems —
mutation), social (learn from NPCs, gain trust), historical (clues across civilizations), Dream (impossible logic),
cosmic (gravity, celestial positioning, dimensions, unfamiliar technology).

## 9. Multiple solutions
Where practical, acknowledge the three power paths — gear repairs the mechanism, mutation manipulates the structure,
arcane restores the seal — plus a fourth **ordinary** solution: find another entrance. Choosing no supernatural power
must never make the game unplayable.

## 10. Power paths change how you adventure
- **Gear** — "give me enough tools and I can solve anything": scanners, gadgets, armour, traversal equipment,
  mechanical interaction, specialized weapons and tools.
- **Mutation** — "my body adapts": strength, climbing, jumping, senses, environmental resistance, regeneration,
  specialized anatomy. The body visibly changes with developed mutations.
- **Arcane** — "the world has rules most people cannot see": magical interaction, enchanted blades, spirits, seals,
  ancient languages, supernatural traversal, Bokai, celestial traditions, other schools.

## 11. No-power play
An ordinary human with money, friends, skills, transport, equipment, intelligence and preparation should still explore
substantial parts of the world. Power expands options; it does not gate participation.

## 12. Companions matter
The player need not own every ability. You may not know magic — your friend does. Not understand machinery — the
dwarf engineer you befriended does. Relationships can become adventure abilities, making friendship mechanically
meaningful without reducing NPCs to equipment.

## 13. Returning rivals
Maya, Jace, Theo, Kira and Blair participate based on relationship, location, their current life, skills, power path
and story progress. They do not wait in a party menu: sometimes they can come, sometimes they are working, sometimes
they meet the player there later.

## 14. Mr. Jones
One of the strongest bridges between ordinary life and adventure: today a repair, later investigating impossible
technology, later still building part of the equipment used to leave the planet. He never becomes irrelevant.

## 15. The Dream Realm
Rare, not another biome. Entering feels different from travelling to a town. It offers extraordinary resources, lost
civilizations, impossible environments, ancient knowledge, powerful enemies, strange puzzles and answers unavailable
elsewhere — with consequences (the Rolling Tides hero's sickness). It is a shortcut to things reality was never
supposed to give easily.

## 16. Cosmic travel
Reaching another planet must not turn this into an unrelated space RPG. The player should still meet ordinary people,
visit towns, buy clothing, eat local food, make friends, learn activities, explore neighbourhoods and find work. The
life simulation travels with the player: a planet is somebody else's home, not Dungeon Planet.

## 17. Ancient Cousins as the bridge
Discovering their origin eventually raises "where is their world?" — a character-creation race that can become an
entire cosmic storyline for players who keep pulling that thread.

## 18. Scale contrast
Monday: work. Tuesday: basketball with Jace. Wednesday: help Mr. Jones test equipment. Thursday: an Egyptian tomb.
Friday: a date. Saturday: a Merged Zone. Much later: another planet — then home, and someone asks whether you are
still coming to the cookout. That contrast is the identity.

## 19. Home always matters
Adventure must never make home mechanically irrelevant. House, family, friends, neighbourhood, favourite stores,
pets, hobbies and routines stay anchors. The universe can become enormous; the player's bedroom still matters.

## 20. Long-term life
Life route: apartment → career → relationship → marriage → home → children → community. Adventure route: local
explorer → traveller → archaeologist → Dream explorer → world traveller → cosmic explorer. They coexist: the game
should let "a parent who has been to another planet" make sense.

## 21. No constant apocalypse
Avoid "finish this quest now or the universe dies" — it destroys the life sim. Large threats can exist without
constant urgency; most mysteries let the player disengage and return. Adventure should come from curiosity more often
than obligation.

## 22. Design test for new locations
Ask both: *why would I want to spend a normal day here?* and *what could I discover beneath the surface?* If a major
location answers only one, develop the other.

## 23. The central promise
Never "you must become the greatest hero in existence". Instead: here is a world much larger than you thought — how
much of it do you want to see? Settle in a town, travel the continent, study ancient civilizations, risk the Dream
Realm, cross the planet, find another world, confront cosmic mysteries — or spend years building a home, making
friends, raising a family, playing music and skateboarding through town. Neither is playing incorrectly.

Rolling Tides asked "what's hiding beneath ordinary life?" This game asks: now that you know how enormous reality is,
what kind of life do you want to live inside it?
