# Story Bible — [Working Title] RPG

Narrative authority for the villain arc, the Loss entity, and their connection to Rolling Tides' lore.
Companion to the design doc (systems/mechanics) and the inherited `ROLLING_TIDES_CLAUDE_HANDOFF.md` (prior game's
canon). Narrative reference — **not** authorization to implement everything at once.

## Timeline & relationship to Rolling Tides
- Rolling Tides 2D is the "before": same cast (Maya, Jace, Theo, Blair, Kira, Mr. Jones, grandparents/uncle),
  culminating in the Hallowed Moon conflict and the Dream boundary cracking/fully opening. Shortened and refocused
  from its original design — less career simulation, more story and life-building, kept mostly within the original
  village/town/city map.
- This RPG is the "after": set once the Dream boundary has fully opened and reshaped the home world. The four player
  paths (gear, mutation, magic, heritage) exist because what leaked through became naturalized, no-drawback abilities
  that ordinary people worldwide began manifesting differently.
- The RPG protagonist is NOT the Rolling Tides hero: a distant family member sharing the same grandparents/uncle
  lineage, moving out to build an independent life as the game begins.
- The Rolling Tides hero is now a rarely appearing, transformed relative with his own life and family.
- The player's heritage reveal (cousin-species/evolved-kin lineage) is latent in the whole family line — planted by
  Rolling Tides' previously unrevealed detail that Grandpa has nonhuman ancestry. Discovered by exploring family
  history, never stated outright.

## The Rolling Tides hero — present-day state
- Naturally a family-building, creative, logical person at his core.
- Repeated use of Dream-derived powers during the climax eroded that side of him; each heroic act replaced more of his
  actual self with an exaggerated "hero" persona.
- Self-reinforcing: the town's collective dreaming of him as "the hero" feeds real energy into the persona. Fame is a
  cage he did not choose.
- Mr. Jones is explicitly safe from this — only goofy, harmless dream-mishaps ("the cameraman, he never dies"). He
  studied the ancient race/Dream lore obsessively as a kid but never went in.
- Counterforce: time and connection with family. Build as a real, high-stakes system, not flavour:
  - collective fan-dreaming reinforces Hero-mode;
  - presence with family reinforces his real self;
  - neglected long enough, he can fully forget he is a family man — a genuinely losable relationship with permanence
    thresholds, not a cosmetic meter.
- His appearances should carry a felt cost, never a free mentor drop-in.
- Thematic function: the clearest embodiment of "coping mechanisms / collective belief overwriting identity", echoing
  Grandpa's split consciousness — except the hero's loss is partly self-inflicted, partly imposed by what others need.

## Dream-influence as an ongoing world mechanic
- Ordinary dreaming remains a channel into the Dream Realm; time spent dreaming carries ambient accumulating risk.
- Powers from what leaked into the natural world are stable, naturalized and drawback-free (the four player paths).
- Direct Dream Realm access is a separate, rare, higher-risk layer: small adventures can offer temporary "forbidden"
  abilities at a cost echoing the hero's fate.

## The four orphans — core villain structure
Four children failed by the people meant to protect them became each other's found family: protected one another,
stole, lied, ran, fought. Their shared promise — to become powerful enough that nobody could hurt them again — is what
destroys them. They pursue an ancient supernatural power (blessing, curse, reality-altering force, wish-granter,
depending on the culture describing it) believing it means freedom. Their mission starts out sounding almost heroic.

The power source is **separate in origin from the Dream Realm** — parallel, unconnected in cause, entangled in effect:
their powers manifest *through* the Dream Realm, filtered through each orphan's defining emotional trait. The Dream
Realm is the medium, not the source.

**Self-reinforcement:** unlike the RT hero (locked in passively by others' belief), the orphans self-reinforce by
repeatedly using their own trait-driven power — an active, self-authored process. Same underlying mechanic, different
cause, different moral weight. They also draw on occult boosts from remaining Hollowed Moon members — individual
sympathisers/suppliers, not leadership; the orphans are a separate group.

### The Leader — Power
- Philosophy: weakness creates suffering, so become stronger than everyone. Expands into: people cannot control their
  own lives, so he should decide how the world functions.
- Tragedy: becomes exactly what he hated — someone powerful deciding how weaker people may live — without noticing.
- The betrayal: the power's source needs one primary vessel; he chooses himself, absorbing/sacrificing/abandoning/
  sealing/stripping the other three. "I'll protect us" becomes "only I can save us"; he never notices the substitution.
- Redemption: permanent, unredeemable. His corruption is self-authored logic, not external manipulation. The
  Leader/Loss transcript found at Envy's death adds ambiguity — keep it textually ambiguous, and never let it excuse
  his culpability.

### Why the other three still follow him
A dysfunctional former family that does not know how to exist apart: Envy believes approval can be earned back; Fear is
terrified of opposing him; Greed believes proximity is the only route to power; Deceit's loyalty is more complicated.

### Fear
- Wound: danger could appear at any moment; obsessive threat-prediction as coping.
- Philosophy: destroy anything that *could* hurt us — possibility is proof enough.
- Manifestation: pre-emptive, anxious, defensive constructs — traps, wards, surveillance-like effects.
- Irony: avoiding every danger has made genuine relationships impossible.
- Optional device: fear of losing things can slide into Greed's identity — coping mechanisms becoming identity.
- Being used by Loss: tricked, not a willing participant. Redemption possible; scene not yet authored.

### Greed
- Wound: chronic scarcity, and things taken away whenever finally received.
- Philosophy: hoard enough and nobody can take everything again — safety, not vanity. Food, weapons, money, artifacts,
  information, magic, territory, eventually people.
- Becomes one of the physically strongest; absorbs supernatural objects. Boss arena can contain recognisable relics
  from earlier in the game as a literal image of how much they have consumed.
- Real fear reframed: not poverty — loss. Being used by Loss; redemption possible. Tragic beat leans emotional: the
  hoard destroyed at once, nothing left, and the player is the one thing that stays. Scene not yet authored.

### Envy
- Wound: connection, not things. The protagonist's earned relationships read as an accusation — why did you get that
  life?
- Powers: emotional manipulation, amplified jealousy, distorted memories, temporary control of people close to the
  protagonist, forced fights against trusted allies.
- Goal: prove the protagonist's relationships are not real — manufactured suspicion, exposed secrets, altered memories,
  faked betrayals. Victory condition: if everyone abandons you, your life was no different from mine.
- Boss encounter: warped familiar locations, accusatory appearances by people from the player's life, some memories
  accurate and some manipulated, party members vanishing; reactive to relationships actually built in play.
  Resolution: those relationships took trust, forgiveness, effort and vulnerability — Envy wanted the result without
  believing the process was real.
- **Ending (most finalised):** Envy joins the Leader believing it is the family they never had. The Leader sends them
  on a mission and has them killed, staged as an accident (a laser strike on their ship returning from a space
  expedition) — the space-travel system becomes the site of death, giving space retroactive stakes. The player finds a
  transcript of the Leader talking with an unnamed voice — this is Loss, unknown to the player: the first, unknowing
  encounter with the true final boss. Deepest layer: the "dark family" was the same conditional, transactional
  non-family they survived as a child, dressed in power instead of poverty. The wound that created Envy is what kills
  them — not really Loss, not really the Leader.
- Redemption status: the only orphan who truly dies if the player fails to intervene in time. Staging (music, pacing,
  Loss's visual presence closing in) should signal the stakes without an explicit warning.

### Deceit
- Wound: truth got them punished; trust got them hurt; manipulation became a survival skill, then a nature.
- Function: infiltrates the protagonist's life as an ally — helpful, likeable, possibly travelling with the player,
  befriending NPCs, sometimes genuinely saving them — while feeding information to the Leader.
- The twist: the friendship may become partially genuine. Deceit may stop knowing which family they belong to. That
  ambiguity is what makes the betrayal land.
- **Redemption (authored setpiece):** Deceit burns out during a fight in jungle ruins; a storm hits immediately after.
  The player can make camp and simply rest there with them until they recover — no lecture, no forgiveness demanded,
  just presence. This sets the tonal template for the others: quiet, vulnerable care right after the edge moment.
- Open: whether Deceit is a townsfolk-level or a prominent character.

## Loss / Remorse — the true final antagonist
- Not one of the orphans: the hidden force behind three of their corruptions (Fear, Greed, Envy). Only the Leader's
  fall is self-authored.
- First encountered unknowingly at Envy's death, via the ambiguous transcript.
- Nature: attacks what the protagonist cannot undo. Requires the player to have experienced a meaningful loss to reach
  full form; condition is the wish that something had happened differently.
- The illusion: grants the wish — the lost person restored, everything seemingly normal.
- Escalating wrongness: misremembered details, a stopped clock, an anachronistic object, repeated conversations,
  evasive answers, NPCs staring. The illusion adapts and lets the player rationalise. Maintaining it visibly costs the
  entity more of the protagonist's energy over time.
- Memory puzzles: escape by reconstructing what actually happened across distorted memories, resolving contradictions
  only with things the player actually experienced — where someone stood, what was said, who was present, what someone
  owned, the sequence of events. The life-sim layer becomes the clue data; this is why those systems are load-bearing.
- The final trap: at the true memory, the illusion's version of the lost person begs them to stay. The protagonist must
  say, in effect, "because you're not really here" — not to stop grieving, but to stop sacrificing reality to avoid it.
- The real battle: little real time has passed; the entity has been draining them. The protagonist returns changed —
  from wanting to undo the past to understanding it cannot be changed and need not control the future.

## Thematic throughline
Every major figure is a different answer to: what do people do with pain?
Fear prevents. Greed collects. Envy resents. Deceit hides. The Leader controls. The RT hero dissolves into the role
others need. Loss offers the ultimate temptation: you do not have to accept what happened.
The protagonist's answer, and the thesis: pain can change you without controlling you. Accepting what happened is not
the same as letting it dictate every future choice.

## Redemption / failure branches
- Fear and Greed: each has an edge moment the player can intervene in. Success starts a redemption arc (Deceit's tent
  scene is the tonal template). Failure does not kill them — they survive as grey-area, solemn vigilantes.
- Envy: the sole permanent-stakes case; failure means real death in the space betrayal.
- Unredeemed orphans periodically reappear in the village, suggesting they may still be reachable — bittersweet, not a
  disguised second chance (leaning: no real second window).
- Unredeemed orphans seek revenge against both the player and the Leader, allied with neither; they may sabotage the
  Leader in ways that incidentally help, or attack the player in ways that incidentally weaken him.
- Endings: all three redeemed restores the found family (three of four plus the player as honorary member, without the
  Leader); partial or none leaves vigilantes in the world. Even a successful ending should carry bittersweetness if
  someone was lost. Outcomes are independent per character (0–3), not capped.

## Open questions

2. Does failing to reach Fear/Greed in time risk failure, or is the encounter guaranteed once triggered?
3. Do village visits from unredeemed orphans ever offer a real second chance? (Leaning: no.)
4. Should redeemed orphans carry visible grief if another was not saved? (Leaning: yes.)
5. Deceit's social position: background character or close to rival-cast prominence?
6. Confirm the Leader/Loss transcript stays permanently ambiguous.
7. What do the orphans give Hollowed Moon in exchange for occult boosts?
8. The RT hero's losable family relationship needs concrete thresholds — specced as a system in the design doc,
   cross-referenced here as narrative stakes.
