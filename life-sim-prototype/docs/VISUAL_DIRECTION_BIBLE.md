# 3D RPG — Visual Direction Bible

## Purpose

This document defines what correct looks like before assets are built.

`RIVAL_CORRECTION_PASS.md` and `CLOTHING_FIT_PASS.md` should be treated as correction/history documents. They describe
problems discovered after implementation. This document is the source of truth for the intended visual identity.

The game's visual language combines three major influences:

1. Rolling Tides / modern city-skater life
2. Medieval-fantasy with a gothic undertone
3. Ancient Egypt and desert mythology

These should not look like three unrelated games. They are different cultural and historical layers interpreted through
the same stylized RPG world.

## 1. Core visual identity

The baseline should feel like a stylized PS2-era adventure RPG translated into a modern 3D indie game.

Characters should be:

- highly readable from gameplay distance
- expressive without becoming overly realistic
- stylized rather than chibi
- capable of wearing believable layered clothing
- recognizable from silhouette alone
- colorful without looking toy-like
- slightly exaggerated in hair, face, footwear, accessories, and clothing shapes
- grounded enough that modern streetwear does not look ridiculous beside fantasy equipment

The player should be able to stand in a city wearing skate shoes, loose pants and a hoodie, later put on fantasy
equipment, and eventually enter an Ancient Egyptian environment without appearing to come from three separate art
styles.

The connective tissue is character proportion, face construction, material treatment, silhouette language and color
philosophy.

## 2. Rolling Tides — city / skater DNA

Rolling Tides should remain the foundation for the game's modern side.

This is not generic contemporary clothing. The influence is specifically a youthful city/suburban/skater culture with
touches of alternative, emo, pop-punk and late-'90s/2000s style.

### Character feeling

Modern characters should look like people who could:

- skateboard through town
- hang around a basketball court
- meet friends outside a convenience store
- play guitar in somebody's garage
- attend a small concert
- walk through a neighborhood at night
- explore somewhere they probably should not be
- accidentally become involved in a much larger fantasy adventure

The fantasy begins with ordinary people. That contrast is important.

### Modern silhouettes

Use recognizable pieces such as: relaxed or baggy jeans, straight-leg pants, cargos, oversized or fitted graphic tees,
hoodies, layered long sleeves, zip-up jackets, skate shoes, high-top sneakers, beanies, chains, wristbands, belts,
backpacks, headphones, simple necklaces, guitar straps, skate equipment.

Avoid making every character look like a fashion model. Clothing should communicate personality and lifestyle. A skater
should look dressed to actually skate. A musician should look like they could actually perform in their clothes. A
tougher rival can have heavier silhouettes. A quieter character can have cleaner or softer shapes.

### Rolling Tides color language

The city side can use: faded denim, charcoal, black, washed red, forest green, muted blue, cream, brown, occasional
brighter accent colors.

The world itself can provide stronger color through signs, stores, sunset lighting, graffiti, sports courts, cars,
vegetation and interiors. The characters do not need to be neon to remain readable.

## 3. The transition into fantasy

The medieval side should feel like the same visual language getting older, stranger and heavier.

Do not suddenly replace the Rolling Tides design language with generic medieval RPG characters. Instead, translate
familiar shapes:

| Modern | Fantasy |
|---|---|
| Hoodie | Hooded travel cloak |
| Skate shoes / high-tops | Strapped leather boots |
| Layered streetwear | Tunic + undershirt + vest + belt layering |
| Cargo pants | Utility trousers with pouches and straps |
| Modern jacket | Short leather traveling coat |
| Backpack | Adventurer's pack |
| Chain / accessories | Charms, talismans and metal ornaments |
| Graphic shirt symbolism | Faction insignias, embroidered symbols and heraldry |

This creates visual continuity.

## 4. Medieval / gothic undertone

The fantasy world should not be relentlessly dark. The gothic influence is an undertone.

During normal life, the world can still contain warm villages, forests, farms, shops, taverns, festivals, friends
joking with one another, bright daylight and colorful fantasy locations.

The gothic language becomes stronger as the story moves toward danger, corruption, the soul, loss and the orphan
storyline.

### Gothic visual vocabulary

Pointed architecture, tall narrow windows, old stone, ironwork, worn statues, ruined churches or temples, candlelight,
dark wood, aged silver, deep red fabrics, blackened metal, old manuscripts, ravens and similar environmental motifs
when appropriate, twisted vegetation, long shadows, ancient burial spaces, damaged religious imagery, underground
chambers.

Avoid covering everything in skulls and spikes. The world becomes unsettling because something beautiful and old has
deteriorated.

## 5. Clothing — fantasy

Fantasy clothing should still respect the character underneath it. Do not create armor as a replacement body. Garments
must look wearable.

Use layers:

```
BODY
 -> undershirt
 -> main garment
 -> outer garment / armor
 -> belt / waist equipment
 -> accessories
```

Possible silhouettes include: fitted tunics, loose traveling shirts, sleeveless outer tunics, leather jackets/jerkins,
short cloaks, long cloaks, fitted trousers, loose adventuring pants, wrapped forearms, gloves, belts, pouches, boots,
partial armor, and full armor for characters whose role actually requires it.

Armor should have thickness and construction. Do not simply inflate the body mesh.

## 6. Ancient Egypt — new visual pillar

Ancient Egypt becomes a major new cultural and environmental influence.

This should NOT simply mean "put pyramids in a desert." Egypt should introduce an entirely new sense of age, scale,
religion, death and civilization.

If the gothic world represents forgotten European-style history, the Egyptian environments should feel even older —
something that makes the characters realize the mysteries they are dealing with existed long before their current
civilization.

## 7. Egyptian world design

The first impression should be: **heat + scale + color + age + mystery.**

Potential environments include: populated river settlements, irrigated farmland, desert roads, monumental temple
complexes, enormous columns, courtyards, markets, mud-brick homes, palace structures, tomb entrances, underground
burial chambers, quarries, ruined settlements, desert cliffs, oasis areas, ancient shrines, necropolises, enormous
statues, partially buried ruins.

Egypt should not be depicted as nothing but empty yellow sand. The living civilization should contrast heavily with its
burial spaces.

### Living Egypt

White and warm plaster, painted walls, linen, wood, pottery, baskets, vegetation, water, blue and turquoise accents,
red, gold, colorful market goods, painted architectural details. It should feel inhabited.

### Ancient / dead Egypt

Tombs and forbidden areas become darker and more surreal: torchlight, narrow corridors, enormous chambers, faded
paintings, sealed doors, damaged statues, burial objects, deep shafts, darkness beyond torch range, sand entering
ancient interiors, traces of gold against otherwise dead spaces.

This is where the Egyptian pillar can naturally connect with the game's gothic undertone.

## 8. Egyptian character design

Do research before constructing historical clothing. Do not create generic "desert fantasy" outfits and call them
Egyptian. The stylization can belong to our game, but the underlying clothing should reference actual Ancient Egyptian
visual culture.

Possible design ingredients include: linen garments, wrapped or draped construction, broad collars, belts and sashes,
sandals, bracelets, arm ornaments, necklaces, protective amulets, head coverings appropriate to the character,
carefully designed eye makeup, gold and colored stone ornamentation for wealthy characters.

Social class and occupation should visibly matter. A laborer, soldier, priest, merchant and elite character should not
be wearing variations of one costume.

## 9. Egyptian color language

Egypt introduces some of the strongest colors in the game.

| Material | Color |
|---|---|
| Sand / limestone | Warm beige, cream, pale stone |
| Nile / faience | Blue, turquoise, blue-green |
| Vegetation | Natural greens around water and cultivated areas |
| Sun / royal wealth | Gold |
| Earth / pottery | Red-brown and ochre |
| Death / underworld | Black, deep blue, extremely dark interiors |
| Linen | Off-white rather than sterile modern white |

The colorful Egyptian elements should become especially striking against desert stone.

## 10. Connecting Egypt to the orphan story

Egypt should contribute to the story's themes rather than functioning as a sightseeing location.

The orphan storyline already deals with destructive human drives such as envy, greed, fear, the desire for power,
betrayal, loss and remorse.

Ancient Egypt provides a natural environment for exploring ideas surrounding death, remembrance, identity, the soul,
judgment, preservation, power surviving beyond a person's lifetime, and rulers attempting to make themselves eternal.

That makes it especially compatible with the larger soul storyline. The Egyptian section can make the heroes realize
that the supernatural forces affecting them have appeared in different names, religions and civilizations throughout
history.

Do not necessarily make Egyptian mythology "wrong" or secretly caused by the game's villains. Instead, it can be
another culture's interpretation of forces that genuinely exist in this universe. That makes the world feel much
larger.

## 11. The three visual eras

**CITY — "This is my life."**
Skateboards. Friends. Basketball. Music. Neighborhoods. Modern clothes. Family. Normal problems.

**GOTHIC FANTASY — "Something is wrong with this world."**
Swords. Forests. Old towns. Ruins. Magic. Churches. Monsters. Soul imagery. The orphan antagonists.

**ANCIENT EGYPT — "This has happened before."**
Temples. Tombs. The Nile. Gods and religious imagery. Burial rituals. Ancient records. Forgotten powers. Evidence that
the mystery predates the heroes' civilization.

That creates escalation without abandoning the original identity.

## 12. Character continuity test

Before approving a major character, perform this test. Render the character in:

1. modern Rolling Tides clothing
2. fantasy travel clothing
3. Egyptian-region clothing or equipment

Hide the environment.

If these look like three different characters from three different games, the visual system has failed. If they clearly
look like the same person adapting to three different places, it has succeeded.

Face, body proportion, hair language and personality must survive every costume change.

## 13. Final art-direction rule

Do not design assets by asking:

- "What would a skater game character look like?"
- "What would a medieval character look like?"
- "What would an Egyptian RPG character look like?"

Instead ask:

> "What would THIS character look like if they lived in, traveled through, or adapted to this part of our world?"

The person comes first. The culture changes their clothing and surroundings. The game's visual language stays intact.
